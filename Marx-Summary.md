# Karl Marx on Value, Surplus Value, and the Laws of Motion of Capitalism: Study Summary

**Source:** Hunt & Lautzenheiser, *History of Economic Thought: A Critical Perspective* (2011), Ch. 9, "Karl Marx" (pp. 202–246).

> **Heads up:** In earlier chapters Hunt explained a thinker and then critiqued them. In this chapter Hunt is mostly *sympathetic*: he explains Marx, defends him against common misreadings, and only criticizes him in a few places. This summary labels **Hunt's own comments** where they come up. Examples marked *"my illustration"* are not in the book. Everything else follows Hunt's text, put in plain words.

---

## 1. The big idea in three sentences

1. **Capitalism is one historical stage, not the natural order.** Earlier economists treated capital, private property, and exchange as eternal. Marx said capitalism is a specific *mode of production* that grew out of feudalism and can be replaced.
2. **Profit comes from production, not exchange.** Workers sell their *labor power* (their capacity to work) for a wage equal to the cost of their subsistence. But they work longer than it takes to produce that subsistence. The extra hours create **surplus value**, which the capitalist keeps. That is the source of profit, interest, and rent.
3. **The drive to accumulate gives capitalism "laws of motion."** Competition forces capitalists to keep accumulating, which leads to **concentration** of capital, a **tendency for the rate of profit to fall**, **crises**, and growing **alienation** of workers.

**Who was Marx?** Karl Marx (1818–1883). His main economic work is *Capital*. Volume 1 came out in 1867. Volumes 2 (1885) and 3 (1894) were pieced together by **Friedrich Engels** from Marx's unfinished drafts. The *Grundrisse* (notebooks from 1857–58) is a rough draft of many of the same ideas.

## 2. Marx's critique of classical economics (pp. 203–206)

Marx built on **Smith and Ricardo** (especially their labor theory of value), quoted **Thompson and Hodgskin** approvingly but critically, took **Mill** seriously as an opponent, and was contemptuous of **Malthus, Bentham, Senior, Say, and Bastiat**.

**Their biggest mistake: no historical perspective.** History has had several *modes of production* (slave society, feudalism, capitalism). To understand capitalism you must separate:
- features **common to all** production (tools, past labor, some kind of property), from
- features **specific to capitalism**.

Mixing these up led to two big confusions:

| Confusion | Who made it | Marx's answer |
|---|---|---|
| **Capital is in all production** | Nearly everyone (except Hodgskin) | Tools and "stored-up labor" exist in every society. What makes them *capital* is a social fact found only in capitalism: they bring **profit to a special class** of owners. |
| **All economics is exchange** | Writers after Ricardo, esp. **Senior and Bastiat** ("political economy is exchange") | If you only look at exchange, everyone looks **equal, free, and in harmony**. That appearance hides the class relation in production. |

**Property and distribution.** Most economists treated private property as sacred and as the only kind of property. Marx said every mode of production has its own form of property (common property was actually older), and **the form of property decides distribution**. So, against **Mill**, production and distribution are *not* separate.

**The "Eden" of exchange.** Marx mocked the market as a paradise of "Freedom, Equality, Property and Bentham": in exchange everyone is a free owner trading equivalents, each looking out only for himself. But step into the "hidden abode of production" and the money-owner becomes the capitalist and the seller of labor power becomes the worker.

## 3. Commodities and value (pp. 206–212)

### 3.1 Use value vs. exchange value
A **commodity** is a product made to be sold. It has two sides:
- **Use value:** the physical qualities that make it useful (satisfies wants). Use values are different *qualities* and can't be compared as quantities.
- **Exchange value:** the ratio at which it trades for other things, usually shown as a **money price**.

**Money** is a special commodity used as the measuring unit (numéraire), a universal equivalent, a medium of exchange, and a way to store wealth.

**Why labor, not utility?** Commodities have three things in common: exchange value, use value, and being made by labor. Marx rejected use value as the cause of price because use values can't be measured against each other and don't reflect the social relations of capitalism. So the common substance is **labor time**.

### 3.2 Marx's special meaning of "value"
For Marx, **value** is not the same as price. It is a **social relation with a size**:
- **Quality:** value only exists when labor is *not directly social*. You and I depend on each other's products but never see it as a relationship; I produce to sell, you buy to consume.
- **Quantity:** values are in proportion to the **labor time needed** to make each commodity.

In **Volume 1** Marx assumes prices are proportional to values (like Ricardo's first approximation) so he can explain profit. He uses "value" and "exchange value" interchangeably there, which confuses readers. **Volume 3** deals with actual prices (see section 6).

### 3.3 Useful labor vs. abstract labor
| | Useful (concrete) labor | Abstract labor |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | The specific kind of work (tailoring, weaving) | Work as general human effort, with all differences ignored |
| What it creates | **Use value** | **Value** (exchange value) |

Two qualifications:
- Only **socially necessary labor time** counts: the time needed under normal conditions with average skill and intensity. *(My illustration: a slow worker who takes 10 hours to make a chair that normally takes 5 does not make a chair worth 10 hours.)*
- **Skilled labor** counts as a multiple of simple labor. The multiple reflects the cost of training.

### 3.4 Social nature of commodity production
Products become commodities only when made to sell, not for the maker's own use. A society dominated by commodities needs three conditions:
1. Specialization (each producer makes one thing).
2. Full separation of use value from exchange value (you can only get what you need by selling what you make).
3. A big market with money as the universal equivalent.

Each producer works alone, yet everyone depends on everyone. So **relations between people appear as relations between things** (prices, markets) that seem to rule people instead of being ruled by them. *(This idea is often called "commodity fetishism"; Hunt describes it without using that term here.)*

**Hunt's point about Smith:** The invisible-hand argument looks only at exchange and thinks utility is created there. Really, **useful labor creates all utility**; exchange is just a requirement of a commodity society. The market's look of harmony says nothing about whether *class* relations are harmonious.

## 4. Where profit comes from: surplus value (pp. 212–222)

### 4.1 Two circuits
| Circuit | Formula | Meaning | Ends when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simple commodity circulation | **C – M – C** | Sell in order to buy. Goal is a use value. | You get the thing you need. |
| Capitalist circulation | **M – C – M′** | Buy in order to sell dearer. M′ > M. | Never: the money is thrown back in again. |

The increase **M′ – M = surplus value**. The endless chase for it is the driving force of capitalism. The capitalist is "capital personified." Marx: the miser is a capitalist gone mad; the capitalist is a rational miser.

### 4.2 Surplus value can't come from exchange
If goods trade **at value**, equivalents swap and no one gains. If one side sells **above or below** value, one side's gain is the other's loss, so there is **no net gain** for the class as a whole. Therefore, the source must be in **production**.

### 4.3 Industrial capital
Merchant capital and interest-bearing capital are **derivative/"parasitic"**: they grab a share of a surplus created elsewhere (which is why they existed even under feudalism). **Industrial capital** is the form that actually creates surplus value:

**M – C … P … C′ – M′**
1. Buy inputs (M → C).
2. Production (P) turns them into a new commodity worth more (C′ > C).
3. Sell (C′ → M′). M′ exceeds M by exactly as much as C′ exceeds C.

### 4.4 Labor vs. labor power (the key idea)
- **Labor power** = the *capacity* to work. This is what the worker sells.
- **Labor** = the actual work done. This is the *use value* of labor power for the buyer.

Labor power is the only commodity whose use **creates more value than it costs**. That gap is surplus value.

**Two conditions for labor power to be a commodity** ("free in the double sense"):
1. The worker is legally free: owns his or her own person and sells labor power only for a limited time (not a slave).
2. The worker is "free" of any means of production, so he or she has nothing else to sell.

**Definition of capitalism:** a commodity society in which a **small class (capitalists) monopolizes the means of production**, and the majority (workers) must either starve or sell labor power. This is **not natural or eternal**; it came from history.

### 4.5 Value of labor power
Like any commodity, its value = the labor time needed to produce (and reproduce) it = the value of the **subsistence of a worker's family** (including children, who will replace the worker). Subsistence is **historical and cultural**, not a biological minimum. Training costs explain wage differences.

### 4.6 Necessary labor and surplus labor
Book's example: the worker's daily subsistence takes **4 hours** to produce; the working day is **10 hours**.
- **Necessary labor** = 4 hours (replaces the value of labor power, i.e., wages).
- **Surplus labor** = 6 hours (creates surplus value for the capitalist).

Wool and looms (raw materials and tools) only **pass on** their existing value to the cloth. Only labor power **adds** new value beyond its own.

### 4.7 Constant and variable capital
- **Constant capital (c):** machines, tools, buildings, raw materials. Passes on only its own value.
- **Variable capital (v):** money spent on labor power. Its value grows in production.
- Value of output = **c + v + s**.

**Rate of surplus value** (= rate of exploitation):

> **s / v = surplus labor / necessary labor**

In the example: 6/4 = **1.5**. For every hour the worker works for himself or herself, 1.5 hours go to the capitalist.

Profit, interest, and rent are all **pieces of surplus value** divided among property owners.

### 4.8 Length of the working day
Since s depends on how long the day is beyond necessary labor, capitalists push to lengthen it to the limit of human endurance. Marx spent a 72-page chapter of *Capital* on the historical struggle over the working day. A "normal working day" was won only through **centuries of struggle**; capital is careless of workers' health "unless under compulsion from society."

**Accumulate!** Surplus value becomes more capital, which makes more surplus value. Marx's summary of capitalism's creed: "Accumulate, accumulate! That is Moses and the prophets!"

## 5. Value as essence, price as appearance (pp. 222–225)

Hunt stresses that Marx's labor theory of value is widely misunderstood:
- Marx's method separates **appearance** (what we see) from **essence** (the deeper cause). Science means finding the essence and showing how it shows up in appearances. *(Hunt uses an analogy: dating rituals vary a lot, but their essence is the universal need for procreation.)*
- Every society must **allocate labor** among tasks. In capitalism that happens through **selling products**. Labor done privately only becomes **social labor** when the product sells. If the leather maker can't sell the leather, that labor was a "private folly."
- So **abstract labor is the essence (substance) of value, and price is its appearance.** Many surface factors (costs, market structure, demand) affect prices, so the link between value and price is not obvious.

## 6. The transformation problem (pp. 225–231)

**Value side:** W = L_d (dead labor, in means of production) + L_n (necessary labor) + L_s (surplus labor)
**Price side:** p = c + v + r(c + v), where r is the average rate of profit

Each piece lines up: price ↔ value, c ↔ dead labor, v ↔ necessary labor, profit ↔ surplus labor.

**The puzzle:**

> rate of profit **r = s / (c + v) = (s/v) / (c/v + 1)**

- **c/v** = the **organic composition of capital** (means of production per worker).
- Competition tends to **equalize s/v** across industries (same wage and workday) **and** to **equalize r** (capital moves to higher profits).
- But **c/v differs** a lot between industries. Then r and s/v can't both be equal everywhere. Contradiction?

**Marx's answer:** Surplus value is **created** in production in proportion to labor used (so created s/v is equal everywhere). But when goods are **sold** at **prices of production**, competition **redistributes** surplus value: from industries with **low** c/v to those with **high** c/v. So:
- High organic composition → price of production **above** value.
- Low organic composition → price of production **below** value.

**Marx's tables (Vol. 3), five industries:** at prices = values, profit rates range 5% to 40%; the average is 22%. After transformation, every industry earns 22%. Marx claimed three things stay true: (1) all profit rates equal, (2) **total prices = total values**, (3) **total profit = total surplus value**.

**The flaw:** Marx transformed **output** prices but left **input** prices at values. (He noticed this himself but thought it wouldn't matter much.) Later mathematical solutions (starting with **Bortkiewicz**) transform both, but then **only two of the three equalities hold**. A huge literature argues over the best solution.

**Hunt's view:** The common charge that Marx made a *logical error* is wrong. The real issue is how persuasive it is that private labor becomes social only as abstract labor expressed in prices, and why prices only roughly reflect it. (He returns to this in Ch. 19.)

## 7. Where capitalism came from: primitive accumulation (pp. 231–234)

Orthodox economists define capital as a collection of **things** (produced means of production). Marx: tools exist in every society, but **capital is a social relation** found only in capitalism. Property law, which seemed to rest on owning your own labor, turns into the capitalist's right to take workers' **unpaid labor**.

**Myth vs. history:**
- **The myth** (Malthus, Say, Senior, Bastiat, even Mill): long ago, frugal hard workers saved and became capitalists; lazy people spent everything and ended up with nothing to sell but themselves. Marx compared this story to original sin in theology.
- **The history:** **force**: conquest, enslavement, robbery. Marx called this **primitive accumulation**. It created both classes at once:
  - **Workers:** peasants violently driven off land (church lands seized, common lands stolen, **enclosures**). Many became beggars and vagabonds, then were whipped and branded by harsh laws into accepting wage discipline.
  - **Capitalists:** big fortunes from American gold and silver, enslavement of native peoples, looting of the East Indies, the African slave trade, colonies, national debt, taxes, and protectionism, all backed by **state power**.
- Marx: "**Force is the midwife** of every old society pregnant with a new one." Capital came into the world dripping with blood and dirt.

## 8. The laws of motion of capitalism (pp. 234–243)

Once capitalism is running, it **reproduces** the separation of workers from the means of production by itself. Capitalists defend "law and order" (the law of private property). **Competition** forces each capitalist to accumulate or be destroyed, so the drive to accumulate feels like an outside, coercive law. Four consequences:

### 8.1 Economic concentration
Wealth ends up in fewer hands because (1) **the strong crush or absorb the weak** in competition, and (2) **better technology raises the minimum capital** needed to stay in business. The gap between a small capitalist class and the large proletariat keeps widening.

### 8.2 Tendency for the rate of profit to fall
- Accumulation raises the **organic composition of capital (c/v)**: machines grow faster than labor.
- Since **r = (s/v) / (c/v + 1)**, a higher c/v (with s/v constant) **lowers r**. Only labor creates surplus value, but profit is figured on *all* capital, so the same surplus is spread over more capital.
- Smith, Ricardo, Mill, and later Keynes also expected a falling profit rate.
- **Not** a fall in total profit or profit share; Marx thought total profit would usually **rise**.

**Four counteracting influences:**
1. Raising the **intensity of exploitation** (speed-ups, "Taylorism"), which raises s/v.
2. Pushing **wages below the value of labor power** (includes "relative overpopulation").
3. **Cheapening the elements of constant capital** (machines get cheaper to produce).
4. **Foreign trade** (higher profits abroad, cheaper necessities for workers). Later disciples built **theories of imperialism** on this (Ch. 13).

So it is a **tendency**, a way to sort opposing forces, **not an empirical prediction**.

**Hunt's criticism:** Marx's biggest gap here was not linking s/v and c/v. New technology that raises c/v can also make workers' goods cheaper, so **real wages and s/v can both rise**, which could even raise r. Also, many 20th-century inventions were "**capital saving**," so c/v may not have risen much even as the physical stock of machines grew enormously.

### 8.3 Sectoral imbalances and crises
- Wages stay near subsistence **not** because of Malthus's population principle (Marx rejected it: each mode of production has its own population laws) but because of the **industrial reserve army** of the unemployed.
- **The cycle:** boom → reserve army used up → wages rise → capitalists bring in **labor-saving machines** → technological unemployment refills the reserve army → but wages are now held down while output floods the market → **not enough consumer demand** to sell the goods and realize profit.
- **Two sectors:** consumer goods and capital goods. Growth needs their exchange to **balance**. After the shift in income from wages to profits, the consumer goods sector has **excess capacity**, cancels investment, the capital goods sector cuts output, layoffs spread, and it spirals into a **general glut** (depression).
- Unlike simple **underconsumption** theories, Marx's cause is a **structural imbalance** between the sectors' capacities and the distribution of income. A crisis can show up first in either sector.
- Result: a roughly **ten-year cycle** of average activity, boom, crisis, and stagnation, driven by the forming and re-forming of the reserve army.

### 8.4 Alienation and the "increasing misery" of the proletariat
- Humans develop themselves through **work**: using tools to shape nature. Under feudalism, exploitation was at least **personal**. Capitalism left "no other nexus" between people than "cash payment."
- **Alienation:** workers feel cut off from their **work**, from **what they produce** (owned by the capitalist), from their **surroundings**, and from **fellow workers**. Work is "external," forced labor; workers feel human only outside work, in "animal" functions like eating and sleeping.
- **Law of increasing misery:** accumulation of wealth at one pole = accumulation of misery, toil, and degradation at the other. Workers become "an appendage of a machine."
- **Important (Hunt stresses this):** this does **not** mean wages fall. In *Capital*, Marx said wages could **rise** as capital accumulates. Higher wages just loosen the "golden chain." "Misery" means growing **alienation** and stunted human development, not falling pay.
- Marx expected the growing, organized working class to revolt: "the expropriators are expropriated," and a cooperative, planned **socialist** system would follow, in **advanced industrial** countries.

### 8.5 Hunt's closing judgment
- Marx did not foresee that repression would channel workers' misery into **apathy, despair, anxiety, isolation**, rather than revolution.
- His analysis of capitalism stands **independently** of his prediction about revolution's timing. No one can predict the future exactly.
- The collapse of the Soviet bloc (1989–91) doesn't disprove Marx: he wrote about **capitalism**, not those economies, so only events in capitalist economies could prove him wrong.

## 9. Key formulas at a glance

| Symbol | Meaning | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| c | constant capital | means of production |
| v | variable capital | wages / labor power |
| s | surplus value | output value − (c + v) |
| s/v | rate of surplus value (exploitation) | surplus labor ÷ necessary labor |
| c/v | organic composition of capital | means of production per worker |
| r | rate of profit | s/(c+v) = (s/v)/(c/v + 1) |
| W | value of a commodity | c + v + s (dead + necessary + surplus labor) |
| p | price of production | c + v + r(c+v) |

**Practice** *(my illustration)*: c = 60, v = 20, s = 20. Then s/v = 1 (100%), c/v = 3, r = 20/80 = 25%, value = 100. If c/v rises to 5 (c = 100) while s/v stays 1: r = 1/(5+1) ≈ 16.7%. **Profit rate falls.**

## 10. Key terms

- **Mode of production:** a historically specific way society organizes production (slave, feudal, capitalist).
- **Commodity:** a product made for sale in the market.
- **Use value / exchange value:** usefulness vs. trading ratio (price).
- **Value (Marx):** the social relation of indirectly social labor; size = socially necessary labor time.
- **Useful labor / abstract labor:** concrete work that makes use values vs. general human labor that makes value.
- **Socially necessary labor time:** time needed under normal conditions with average skill and intensity.
- **C–M–C / M–C–M′:** selling to buy vs. buying to sell dearer.
- **M–C…P…C′–M′:** the circuit of industrial capital.
- **Surplus value:** M′ − M; value created by unpaid surplus labor.
- **Labor power vs. labor:** capacity to work (sold) vs. actual work (performed).
- **"Free in the double sense":** legally free, and free of (lacking) means of production.
- **Necessary / surplus labor:** hours that replace wages vs. hours that create surplus value.
- **Constant / variable capital:** means of production vs. labor power.
- **Rate of surplus value (s/v):** degree of exploitation.
- **Organic composition of capital (c/v).**
- **Price of production:** cost + average profit; deviates from value.
- **Transformation problem:** turning values into prices of production consistently.
- **Dead labor / living labor:** labor embodied in inputs vs. labor done now.
- **Merchant / interest-bearing capital:** derivative forms that share surplus but don't create it.
- **Primitive accumulation:** the violent historical creation of the two classes.
- **Industrial reserve army:** the unemployed who keep wages down.
- **Concentration:** capital ending up in fewer hands.
- **Falling rate of profit / counteracting influences.**
- **Sectoral imbalance:** mismatch between consumer and capital goods sectors that causes crises.
- **Alienation:** workers estranged from their work, product, and each other.
- **Law of increasing misery:** worsening alienation as capital accumulates (not falling wages).

## 11. Likely exam questions (with short answers)

1. **Why can't surplus value come from exchange?** Equal exchanges create no gain; unequal ones only shift value from one person to another, with no net gain overall.
2. **What's the difference between labor and labor power?** Labor power is the capacity to work, sold for a wage equal to subsistence value. Labor is the work actually done, which creates more value than the wage.
3. **What two conditions define capitalism?** Workers are legally free to sell their labor power and have no means of production, which are monopolized by a capitalist class.
4. **If subsistence takes 4 hours and the day is 10, what is s/v?** 6/4 = 1.5 (150%).
5. **Why is constant capital "constant"?** It only transfers its own value to the product; it adds no new value.
6. **What is the transformation problem?** Equal profit rates plus different organic compositions mean prices of production must differ from values. Marx's solution transformed outputs but not inputs; full solutions keep only two of his three equalities.
7. **What did Marx mean by primitive accumulation, and whom was he criticizing?** The violent creation of a propertyless working class and a wealthy capitalist class; he was mocking the "frugal elite" story of Malthus, Say, Senior, Bastiat, and Mill.
8. **Why does the profit rate tend to fall, and what can offset it?** Rising c/v spreads the same surplus over more capital. Offsets: more intense exploitation, wages below value, cheaper constant capital, foreign trade.
9. **How does Marx's crisis theory differ from simple underconsumption?** The cause is a structural imbalance between the capital-goods and consumer-goods sectors relative to income distribution.
10. **Does the "law of increasing misery" mean wages fall?** No. Marx said wages might rise; misery means growing alienation.
11. **How did Marx explain wages near subsistence without Malthus?** The industrial reserve army of unemployed workers, created by accumulation and labor-saving machinery.
12. **What is Hunt's main criticism of the falling-rate-of-profit argument?** Marx didn't connect c/v to s/v: technical change can raise both real wages and s/v, and capital-saving innovations can keep c/v from rising.

## 12. How this chapter connects

- **Smith & Ricardo (Ch. 3, 5):** Marx extends their labor theory of value and their idea that profit is a deduction from labor's product, and uses Ricardo's "first approximation" of prices proportional to labor. Ricardo also expected prices of capital-intensive goods to be above labor values, as Marx did.
- **Malthus (Ch. 4):** Marx rejects the population principle; the reserve army replaces it.
- **Bentham, Say, Senior (Ch. 6):** Targets of the "Freedom, Equality, Property and Bentham" jab, and of the abstinence/frugal-elite story of capital.
- **Thompson & Hodgskin (Ch. 7):** Early socialist uses of Ricardo; Hodgskin alone saw that capital isn't universal.
- **Bastiat & Mill (Ch. 8):** Bastiat is the clearest case of "political economy is exchange." Mill is criticized for separating production from distribution, though Marx agrees with him on cultural subsistence and on foreign trade raising profits.
- **Coming up:** Ch. 10 (Jevons, Menger, Walras) is the utility-based reply; Ch. 13 covers imperialism theories; Ch. 19 returns to the transformation problem.
